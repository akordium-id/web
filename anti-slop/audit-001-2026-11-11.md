# Audit 001: Akordium Web content drafts

**Date:** 2026-11-11  
**Scope:** 12 drafts, six topics in Indonesian and English  
**Status:** Initial audit. Approved revisions are recorded in the [follow-up report](audit-001-follow-up-2026-11-11.md).

This audit identifies writing patterns that can make copy feel generic or machine-smoothed. It does not determine whether AI wrote any draft. Formal business language is not itself an AI signal.

## Voice direction

The 2023 writing samples use first-person narration, concrete events, varied sentence lengths, and occasional asides or self-correction. For Akordium, keep the useful part of that voice: clear opinions grounded in a real situation. Do not copy the casual jokes or personal storytelling into business copy by default. A professional version can sound human by explaining how a business decision is made, using a real example when one is available, and naming the person responsible for the action.

The drafts already do several things well: they avoid invented customer proof and performance promises, explain business decisions before technical choices, and frequently acknowledge limits in the available data. Preserve those qualities.

## Findings

### 1. High: em dash punctuation appears across ten drafts

The punctuation recurs in both languages and across several sections, which makes the copywriting pattern conspicuous and violates the project's copy rule. Replace it with a comma, colon, parentheses, or a new sentence. This is a mechanical cleanup, not evidence that the underlying writing is AI-generated.

Examples appear in [fleet-coordination-chat-spreadsheets.md](../content-drafts/id/fleet-coordination-chat-spreadsheets.md), [fleet-dashboard-kpis-workflows.md](../content-drafts/en/fleet-dashboard-kpis-workflows.md), [fleet-geofence-operational-exceptions.md](../content-drafts/en/fleet-geofence-operational-exceptions.md), and [gps-tracking-small-fleet-readiness.md](../content-drafts/id/gps-tracking-small-fleet-readiness.md). The two pilot-checklist drafts are the exceptions; no instance was found in those files.

### 2. Medium: repeated contrast-and-caveat framing

Many openings or section conclusions first reject a simplistic answer, then introduce the qualified answer: a website cannot guarantee inquiries, a tracker does not automatically solve delays, a pilot is not automatically small or safe, and a location event does not prove a business process is complete. These distinctions are useful, but the repeated rhetorical shape starts to sound templated across the series.

Keep the caveat when it corrects a real misconception. Elsewhere, lead with the business decision or action, then explain the limitation only if it changes that decision. Examples: [logistics-website-quote-requests.md](../content-drafts/id/logistics-website-quote-requests.md), [fleet-system-pilot-checklist.md](../content-drafts/id/fleet-system-pilot-checklist.md), and [gps-tracking-small-fleet-readiness.md](../content-drafts/id/gps-tracking-small-fleet-readiness.md).

### 3. Medium: the six topics share a highly uniform article recipe

Most drafts move from a cautionary introduction to a set of questions or steps, then a checklist or evaluation section, followed by a consultation CTA. The structure is sensible for practical business guidance, but repeating it for every topic makes the series feel assembled from one template. Keep checklists where readers need to verify readiness, such as the pilot and launch articles. For other topics, consider ending with a decision example, a short “when this is useful” section, or a direct summary instead.

This pattern is visible in the logistics, pilot, geofence, and GPS-readiness drafts, for example [logistics-website-quote-requests.md](../content-drafts/id/logistics-website-quote-requests.md) and [fleet-geofence-operational-exceptions.md](../content-drafts/id/fleet-geofence-operational-exceptions.md).

### 4. Medium: the Akordium CTA repeats the same invitation

All six Indonesian drafts and all six English drafts end with a conditional invitation to discuss a workflow or need with Akordium Lab. The topic-specific wording helps, so the CTA is not generic in the usual “learn more” sense. The repeated placement and construction still make it predictable.

Keep the consultation invitation, but make the next step specific to each article and avoid promising a deliverable that Akordium has not confirmed. The existing “pemetaan workflow” wording in [fleet-coordination-chat-spreadsheets.md](../content-drafts/id/fleet-coordination-chat-spreadsheets.md) is a useful direction.

### 5. Medium: the personal voice is mostly absent

The drafts are clear and appropriately formal, but their voice comes mostly from neutral instructions and abstract examples. The 2023 samples feel personal because the author describes what happened, what they noticed, and how they changed their mind. For business copy, use that principle without importing the casual register: add a real observation from client or internal work only when it can be verified, or state the author's considered view directly. Do not turn the explicitly hypothetical fleet example into an implied client case.

The current hypothetical example is responsibly labeled in [fleet-coordination-chat-spreadsheets.md](../content-drafts/id/fleet-coordination-chat-spreadsheets.md). Keep that label unless a real case is available and approved for publication.

## Recommended revision order

1. Remove the punctuation issue in the ten affected files.
2. Reduce repeated contrast-and-caveat openings across the six topics.
3. Vary article endings and CTA phrasing while keeping the business action clear.
4. Add verified, specific author perspective where available; retain hypothetical labels otherwise.

No evidence of fabricated metrics, customer testimonials, or performance claims was found in the reviewed drafts.
